Article 01 · Leadership & Attention

Attention Is Strategy

In brief

Leadership attention is finite. Where it goes shapes the institution's future.

The most valuable resource in any organization is not capital. It is attention. Every leader has the same 24 hours. Every institution has finite leadership attention.

Yet many organizations unintentionally spend that attention on urgent issues rather than important ones — chasing emails, resolving avoidable problems, and reacting to the crisis of the day. Attention is a strategic resource. Where leaders direct it determines what the organization improves, what it neglects, and ultimately what it becomes.

The highest-performing institutions are intentional about this. They protect leadership time for the decisions that shape the future: Building capability rather than fixing recurring problems. Strengthening systems rather than relying on heroics. Developing people rather than managing every task. Looking beyond the next quarter to the next decade.

Protect time for the future

Article 02 · Strategic Thinking

Ask Better Questions

In brief

Better questions reveal assumptions, risks and opportunities, then improve decisions.

The future will not be led by the organizations with the most data. It will be led by those that ask the best questions. We often hear that data is the new currency. Yet data, on its own, has never made a strategic decision.

The real differentiator is the quality of the questions leaders ask. Good questions challenge assumptions. They uncover risks before they become crises. They reveal opportunities others fail to see. And they encourage cultures where learning matters more than certainty.

The Old Question

Do we have enough information?

The Better Question

Are we asking the questions that truly matter?

Strategic Thinking · Continue the reflection

Judgment makes the difference

As artificial intelligence becomes more capable, access to information will become increasingly equal. What will remain unequal is judgment. The leaders and institutions that will excel are those that combine data with curiosity, technology with wisdom, and analytics with sound governance.

The question is no longer, “Do we have enough information?” It is, “Are we asking the questions that truly matter?” Because the quality of our questions ultimately shapes the quality of our decisions. And the quality of our decisions shapes the future of our institutions.

Article 03 · Decision Quality

Better Decisions at Speed

In brief

Institutional capability reduces the distance between insight and a sound decision.

In the coming decade, competitive advantage will belong to institutions that can make high-quality decisions faster — not simply those that move faster. Speed alone is not a strategy. Making quick decisions with poor judgment only accelerates the wrong outcome.

The real challenge for leaders is reducing the time between insight and sound decision-making without compromising governance, accountability, or strategic thinking. This is where institutional capability matters. Organizations that excel are building: Clear decision rights. Trusted data and intelligence. Simple governance frameworks. Empowered leaders at every level. A culture that values evidence over hierarchy.

Decision Quality at Speed

Keep judgment at the centre

Artificial intelligence will make information more accessible than ever. But it will not replace judgment, integrity, or accountability. These remain uniquely human responsibilities.

The institutions that will define the future are those that combine technology with disciplined leadership—where decisions are not only faster, but consistently wiser. In an age of acceleration, the true competitive advantage is not speed. It is the ability to make better decisions at speed.

Article 04 · Technology & Leadership

Institutional Intelligence

In brief

The lasting differentiator is the ability to turn information into collective judgment and action.

The next competitive advantage will not come from technology alone. It will come from institutional intelligence. Artificial intelligence is transforming how we analyze data, automate processes, and generate insights. Yet as these capabilities become widely available, technology itself will become less of a differentiator.

What will distinguish leading organizations is their ability to convert information into institutional intelligence. Institutional intelligence is the collective capacity to: Connect data across functions rather than operate in silos. Learn from every decision, success, and failure. Anticipate emerging risks before they become crises. Turn insights into timely, measurable action.

Technology

Accelerates decisions

Institutional Intelligence

Ensures those decisions are the right ones

Technology & Leadership · Continue the reflection

Turn information into capability

This requires more than digital transformation. It demands a transformation in governance, culture, and leadership. The institutions that will lead the next decade will not necessarily be those with the most advanced AI platforms. They will be those that combine technology with sound judgment, trusted data, clear accountability, and a commitment to continuous learning.

Technology can accelerate decisions. Institutional intelligence ensures those decisions are the right ones. As AI becomes increasingly accessible, perhaps the defining question for leaders is no longer, “How intelligent is our technology?” It is, “How intelligent is our institution?”

Article 05 · Leadership Practice

The Attention Audit

In brief

A practical exercise based on July's leadership article: turn a completed week into a clearer choice about the next one.

Choose one completed working week. Review your calendar alongside your work records, then assign each block of time to one of four categories: essential operations, recurring problem-solving, people development, or institution building.

Record the minutes actually spent. Label estimates where the duration is uncertain, count each minute only once, and identify any unrecorded time. Use one primary category for each block, even when the activity served several purposes.

Calculate the institution-building share by dividing the minutes in that category by all recorded working minutes, then multiplying by 100. This is a personal baseline for reflection, not a universal performance target. The four categories are a suggested review tool.

Design the Next Week

Leadership Practice · Continue the reflection

Make the next week different

Review recurring problems. Select one that a better process, clearer authority or more reliable information could resolve. Name an owner and evidence that repeated intervention is no longer necessary.

Protect one block next week for an important decision about the institution’s future. Decide what to stop, defer or delegate while maintaining essential services. Define the result this time should produce.

Repeat the review next week. Compare recorded time, recurring issues and the promised result. Keep changes that build capability; adjust those that do not.

Make one improvement that reduces tomorrow's need for intervention.

Review Categories

Where did the week go?

  • Operationsessential delivery
  • Recurring issuesrepeated intervention
  • Peopledevelopment
  • Institutionfuture capability

Key takeaways

  • Use recorded time as a baseline; label estimates and avoid double counting.
  • Address one recurring cause with a named owner and visible evidence.
  • Protect time for the future, then review the result it produces.

Article 06 · Leadership Practice

The Decision Brief

In brief

A practical exercise based on July's articles: make the question, evidence, authority and learning visible before and after a decision.

Choose an important decision. State what must be decided and by when. Use five brief sections: question, evidence, options, authority and review. Separate facts from assumptions and name evidence that could change your view.

Compare at least two feasible options using the same criteria: strategic fit, ability to deliver and risk. Consider deferral only if realistic, with explicit consequences. Use qualitative judgments when evidence cannot support precise scores.

Name the authorised decision owner and required consultees. Define what new information or risk would trigger escalation. Adapt these suggested steps to the institution’s existing governance.

Before the Decision

Question, evidence and expected result

At the Review

Actual result, assumptions and lesson

Seven Lines · 18 / 22

Leadership Practice · Continue the reflection

Build a learning record

Record the chosen option, its rationale and the main remaining uncertainty. Define one observable progress indicator and a review date. Make the reasoning understandable to another responsible colleague.

At review, compare actual and expected outcomes. Which assumptions held or failed? What should change next time? Record the lesson in a reusable decision log with appropriate access permissions.

Use technology to organise questions and evidence while retaining human judgment and accountability. Keep the brief concise and actionable; expand details when the consequences or uncertainty justify it.

A useful decision record preserves both the choice and the reason.

Brief Sections

A clear brief before a clear decision

  • Questionwhat and by when
  • Evidencefacts and assumptions
  • Optionsfeasible choices
  • Authorityowner and consultation
  • Reviewresult and learning

Key takeaways

  • Define the decision and distinguish facts from assumptions.
  • Compare feasible options under clear authority and consultation.
  • Record the reasoning, review the result and retain the lesson.
