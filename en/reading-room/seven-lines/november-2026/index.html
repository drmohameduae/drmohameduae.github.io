Climate Adaptation and Public Investment

2 November 2026

The most expensive infrastructure project may be the one we approve without considering tomorrow's climate.

Governments invest billions in roads, hospitals, ports, water networks, housing and other critical infrastructure.

Yet an important question deserves greater attention:

Are we investing for the climate of yesterday, or preparing for the realities of tomorrow?

As COP31 approaches, the global conversation on climate adaptation must evolve beyond funding commitments and resilience strategies.

We need to reconsider how public investment decisions are made.

Infrastructure designed to serve communities for the next 30 or 50 years must account for conditions that may be fundamentally different from those experienced today.

Rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, water scarcity, coastal risks and extreme weather are no longer considerations that can be addressed solely through environmental policies.

They are increasingly relevant to fiscal sustainability, economic competitiveness and national security.

This calls for a significant shift in public-sector governance.

Climate resilience should become a mandatory consideration in capital investment planning, procurement, infrastructure design and long-term asset management.

For every major public investment, decision-makers should understand not only the initial construction cost, but also its long-term exposure, vulnerability, operating requirements and potential adaptation costs.

A cheaper project today may become a considerably more expensive liability tomorrow.

This is particularly relevant to the Arabian Gulf, where extreme heat, water constraints, coastal development and rapid urban expansion create interconnected challenges.

Technology, predictive modelling, environmental intelligence and scenario analysis can help governments make better-informed decisions.

But technology alone cannot deliver resilience.

It requires institutional accountability, coordinated planning and the willingness to integrate future risks into today's economic decisions.

Perhaps the next evolution of climate governance is therefore not another standalone adaptation strategy.

It is ensuring that every significant investment decision becomes an opportunity to strengthen national resilience.

After all, the true cost of infrastructure is not simply what we spend to build it.

It is what society ultimately pays when we fail to prepare it for the future.

The Infrastructure Bottleneck Behind the Energy Transition

9 November 2026

The energy transition may not be constrained by our ability to generate clean electricity. It may be constrained by our ability to deliver it.

As COP31 opens today in Antalya, the world will once again focus on emissions targets, renewable energy investments and climate commitments.

Yet an important infrastructure challenge deserves greater attention.

According to the International Energy Agency, more than 2,500 GW of electricity generation, storage and large-demand projects are currently stalled in grid connection queues worldwide.

This highlights a fundamental reality:

Building renewable energy capacity is not the same as building an effective energy transition.

Solar panels and wind turbines generate electricity. But grids, storage, digital intelligence and institutional coordination determine whether that electricity can reliably support economic activity.

Meanwhile, artificial intelligence, data centres, industrial electrification, electric vehicles and growing cooling requirements are reshaping electricity demand.

The challenge is no longer simply how much clean energy we can produce.

It is whether our infrastructure can accommodate the scale, complexity and speed of tomorrow's economy.

For governments, this calls for a different approach to energy planning.

Electricity networks should be treated as strategic national infrastructure, with investment decisions informed by future demand, economic diversification, technological change and climate resilience.

Regulatory frameworks must enable faster connections without compromising system reliability.

Regional interconnections should be evaluated as opportunities to strengthen security and efficiency.

And intelligent demand management, energy storage and digital technologies must become integral components of national energy strategies.

For the Arabian Gulf, where ambitious economic transformation programmes intersect with increasing electricity demand, these considerations are particularly relevant.

The next competitive advantage may not belong to the countries that produce the most electricity, but to those that manage and deliver it most intelligently.

As COP31 begins, the question policymakers should consider is not simply how much additional renewable capacity countries will install.

It is whether their energy systems are being transformed quickly enough to accommodate it.

Because ultimately:

The success of the energy transition will be determined not only by what we generate, but by what our infrastructure enables.

Sustainable Cooling and National Resilience

16 November 2026

In a warming world, cooling is no longer a matter of comfort. It is becoming a matter of national resilience.

When we discuss climate adaptation, attention typically turns to infrastructure protection, water security, coastal resilience and disaster preparedness.

Yet one of the most consequential challenges facing governments is often overlooked.

How will we keep our cities, economies and communities safely functioning as temperatures continue to rise?

According to UNEP's Global Cooling Watch 2025, global cooling demand could more than triple by 2050 under current trends.

For countries in the Arabian Gulf, this is not a distant environmental concern.

Cooling already plays a fundamental role in our buildings, healthcare facilities, industries, food supply chains and everyday economic activity.

The conventional response to increasing temperatures has largely been to expand air-conditioning capacity.

But this raises an important strategic question:

Should we continue investing primarily in cooling our buildings, or should we fundamentally rethink how we design our cities to require less cooling in the first place?

The distinction has profound implications for energy security, economic competitiveness, public health and environmental sustainability.

Future cooling strategies must integrate urban planning, building design, energy efficiency and climate adaptation into a single policy framework.

This means designing buildings that reduce heat gain, strengthening energy-performance standards, expanding efficient district cooling where appropriate, investing in passive cooling and urban shading, and developing electricity systems capable of managing extreme summer demand.

It also means recognizing that heat resilience extends beyond buildings.

Outdoor workers, vulnerable communities, public spaces and critical infrastructure all require appropriate protection.

For governments, sustainable cooling should therefore evolve from a technical building requirement into a strategic national infrastructure and public-policy priority.

And the real opportunity is not simply to reduce electricity consumption.

It is to reduce long-term infrastructure expenditure, strengthen public health, improve urban liveability and enhance national economic resilience.

As COP31 places renewed attention on sustainable cooling, perhaps we should reconsider how we measure progress.

The ultimate achievement will not be how much additional cooling capacity we install.

It will be how effectively we create cities and economies that remain liveable, productive and resilient while requiring fewer resources to stay cool.

That is where climate adaptation becomes intelligent development.
