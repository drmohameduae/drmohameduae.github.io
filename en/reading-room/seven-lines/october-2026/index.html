Article 01 · Urban Policy

Housing and Liveable Cities

In brief

A city's sustainability begins with whether its residents can afford to live there.

A city cannot call itself sustainable if people cannot afford to live in it.

Today, as the world marks World Habitat Day 2026, we should perhaps broaden the way we think about housing. Adequate housing is not simply a social policy issue. It is an economic issue, an environmental issue, an infrastructure issue and ultimately a question of human dignity.

Where and how we build homes determines commuting patterns, energy consumption, pressure on water and infrastructure, exposure to climate risks, access to employment and, ultimately, quality of life. A poorly planned city can build thousands of homes and still deepen inequality. A well-planned city connects housing with transport, public space, services, employment, environmental quality and resilience.

This is why the next generation of urban policy must move beyond counting housing units. We should ask: Are we building communities—or simply buildings?

The distinction matters. Housing that is affordable but disconnected from employment and services creates another form of cost. Housing that ignores climate risk creates tomorrow’s vulnerability.

Urban development that eliminates natural systems may solve one problem while creating several others.

Housing units alone

Building Communities

Homes linked to jobs and services

Urban Policy · Continue the reflection

Assess the whole neighbourhood

And regeneration that improves neighbourhoods but displaces the people who already live there cannot truly be described as inclusive. The cities that succeed in the decades ahead will be those that treat housing as part of an integrated urban system—connecting land, mobility, energy, water, nature, infrastructure and economic opportunity. For our region, where cities continue to grow and evolve rapidly, there is an extraordinary opportunity to demonstrate that urban development can be ambitious without sacrificing liveability, environmental responsibility or social inclusion.

Perhaps the measure of a successful city should therefore not be how impressive its skyline becomes. It should be how well people are able to live beneath it.

Are we building communities—or simply buildings?

Article 02 · Food and Water Security

Food Security and Resilience

In brief

Resilient food systems protect the resources they need to nourish future generations.

The future of food security will not be determined by how much food we produce, but by how intelligently we manage the systems that sustain it.

For decades, food security has often been discussed primarily in terms of agricultural production, imports and strategic reserves. But the challenge is considerably more complex. A country may increase domestic food production while simultaneously placing unsustainable pressure on its groundwater, energy resources and natural ecosystems.

Equally, a country may have limited agricultural land yet achieve greater food resilience through innovation, diversification, technology and sound resource management. This raises a fundamental question: Are we pursuing food security—or merely increasing food production?

Securing Food Systems

Seven Lines · 6 / 20

Food and Water Security · Continue the reflection

Plan for a resilient food system

The distinction is particularly important for arid regions, including the Arabian Gulf, where water availability, climatic conditions and environmental constraints demand a fundamentally different approach. The next generation of food security strategies must integrate five critical dimensions: Water security: Ensuring that agricultural development does not compromise the availability of water for future generations.

Technological innovation: Applying precision agriculture, artificial intelligence and advanced production technologies where they deliver measurable benefits. Supply-chain resilience: Diversifying sources, strengthening logistics and reducing exposure to external disruptions. Resource efficiency: Minimizing food loss and waste while improving energy, land and water productivity. Environmental sustainability: Protecting the ecosystems and natural resources upon which long-term food production ultimately depends. As we approach World Food Day 2026, under the theme “Innovate today. Nourish tomorrow,” we should recognize that innovation alone is insufficient. Innovation must be supported by integrated policies, intelligent investment, institutional coordination and measurable outcomes.

For governments, the challenge is not simply to produce more food domestically. It is to establish a food system capable of ensuring availability, affordability, nutritional quality and resilience without compromising environmental sustainability. Perhaps the most important measure of food security is therefore not how much we can produce today.

It is how reliably we can nourish future generations without exhausting the resources they will inherit. That is where food security becomes a matter of national resilience and responsible governance.

Are we pursuing food security—or merely increasing food production?

  • Protect water before scaling output.

Article 03 · Economic Governance

Biodiversity Economics

In brief

Public finance and investment can support conservation or work against it.

The most consequential environmental decisions are not always made by environment ministries. Sometimes, they are made by finance ministries.

As the world gathers in Yerevan today for COP17, the United Nations Biodiversity Conference, we should reconsider a fundamental assumption about environmental protection. For decades, biodiversity conservation has largely been approached through environmental legislation, protected areas, restoration programmes and conservation funding. These interventions remain essential.

However, there is a deeper structural challenge. What happens when governments invest in protecting nature while other economic policies simultaneously encourage its degradation? We cannot expect environmental institutions to reverse biodiversity loss if prevailing financial incentives continue to reward activities that undermine ecosystems.

Projects protect selected habitats

Aligning Finance

Seven Lines · 9 / 20

Economic Governance · Continue the reflection

Align incentives with outcomes

This is precisely why the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework includes a commitment to reduce harmful incentives by at least US$500 billion annually by 2030. The implication is significant. Biodiversity loss is not merely an environmental management problem. It is also an economic governance problem.

Consider the relationship between public investment, infrastructure development, agricultural subsidies, industrial policy, water allocation and natural resource management. Each can either reinforce environmental sustainability or undermine it. The difference often lies in how economic incentives are structured.

This requires a fundamental shift in institutional thinking. Environmental protection should no longer be regarded primarily as expenditure. It should increasingly be understood as an investment in the natural systems that sustain economic productivity, public health, water security, food systems and national resilience.

For arid regions, including the Arabian Gulf, this perspective is particularly important. Groundwater reserves, coastal ecosystems, marine biodiversity and fragile terrestrial habitats represent natural assets whose degradation can generate substantial long-term economic and social consequences. The opportunity lies in integrating their value into investment decisions, fiscal policies, infrastructure planning and national development strategies.

The objective should not be to constrain economic development. It should be to ensure that economic development does not systematically undermine the natural capital upon which future prosperity depends. As COP17 begins, perhaps the most important question for policymakers is not simply how much additional financing can be mobilized for biodiversity. It is whether existing financial systems are aligned with the environmental outcomes governments have committed to achieving. Because ultimately, a nation's commitment to nature is reflected not only in what it chooses to protect. It is equally reflected in what its economic policies encourage.

The difference often lies in how economic incentives are structured.

Article 04 · Infrastructure and Nature

Natural Infrastructure

In brief

Healthy ecosystems underpin economic activity and should be managed as national assets.

Not all essential infrastructure is built. Some of it is alive.

When governments discuss infrastructure resilience, the conversation typically revolves around roads, bridges, power networks, ports and water systems. Yet some of our most valuable infrastructure receives considerably less attention. Coral reefs, mangroves, wetlands and seagrass meadows quietly perform functions that support economic activity, protect coastlines, sustain fisheries and strengthen community resilience.

They are not merely environmental assets. They are natural infrastructure. A new global assessment by the IUCN, released during COP17, warns that 96% of assessed shallow-water coral reef ecosystems could face an extremely high risk of collapse by 2075 under its modelled warming scenario.

This should concern more than environmental authorities.

Seven Lines · 12 / 20

Infrastructure and Nature · Continue the reflection

Manage ecosystems as assets

It should concern economic policymakers, infrastructure planners, investment institutions and national security strategists. Consider how we manage conventional infrastructure. We inspect bridges before they deteriorate.

We monitor electricity networks to anticipate failures. We invest in preventive maintenance to avoid expensive disruptions. Yet natural ecosystems are often managed reactively, receiving attention only when degradation becomes visibly severe.

What if we applied the same strategic discipline to natural infrastructure that we apply to engineered infrastructure? This would require integrating ecosystem health into national asset management, infrastructure planning and investment decisions. It would mean establishing measurable ecological performance indicators, investing in continuous environmental monitoring and assigning clear institutional accountability for the condition of critical ecosystems.

For coastal economies, particularly across the Arabian Gulf, this approach is especially relevant. Our coastlines support economic development, maritime activities, tourism, fisheries and communities. The ecosystems protecting and sustaining these activities should not be treated as secondary considerations.

Restoration and conservation remain essential, but prevention must receive equal strategic attention. Neither can replace the need to address climate change. Ultimately, the question is not simply how much we are willing to invest in protecting nature. It is whether we fully understand the economic and societal consequences of allowing natural systems to deteriorate.

The next generation of infrastructure planning must recognise that protecting nature is not separate from protecting prosperity. Because some of the most important assets a nation possesses were never constructed. They evolved over thousands of years— and cannot easily be replaced.

They are natural infrastructure.

The Leadership Lab

The Resilience Scorecard

Proposed review activity. Work through the hypothetical scenario, then compare your response with the guide below.

  1. Hypothetical case: A city proposes homes and a food hub beside a natural coastal buffer.
  2. For one year, the team can fund either a preventive redesign or downstream repairs.
  3. Name one test each for home access, food supply and the buffer’s health.
  4. Pick and defend a choice. Name a funding condition, who monitors it and what evidence could reverse your choice.
Answers and discussion

Discussion guide: Test access to jobs, backup food routes and the buffer’s health. Prefer redesign if it protects the buffer and keeps homes accessible. Set a safeguard and review the evidence.
