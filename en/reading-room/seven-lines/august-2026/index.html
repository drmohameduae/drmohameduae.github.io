Article 01 · Sustainability & Governance

Sustainability in Decisions

In brief

Sustainability becomes transformative when it shapes everyday decisions, resources and accountability.

Sustainability becomes transformative only when it moves from the margins of strategy to the centre of decision-making. Too often, sustainability is treated as a separate programme, report, or department.

But the most resilient institutions understand that it must influence how investments are selected, risks are assessed, operations are designed, people are developed, and performance is measured.

A sustainability strategy cannot succeed if the wider organization continues to make decisions using only short-term financial or operational considerations. Real progress begins when environmental and social impact becomes part of everyday governance.

Separate Programme

An additional responsibility

Everyday Governance

A better way of making decisions

Ask better questions

This means asking better questions: Will this decision strengthen long-term resilience? Are we solving a problem—or merely transferring its consequences elsewhere? Can economic value, environmental responsibility, and social benefit be created together? The future will not reward organizations that simply communicate ambitious commitments. It will reward those that embed sustainability into their systems, incentives, budgets, and accountability frameworks. Sustainability is not an additional responsibility. It is a better way of making decisions.

Sustainability is not an additional responsibility. It is a better way of making decisions.

Questions

Three questions before a decision

  • ResilienceStronger over time?
  • ConsequencesSolved or transferred?
  • Shared valueEconomic · environmental · social

Key takeaways

  • Bring sustainability into everyday governance.
  • Consider long-term resilience and transferred consequences.
  • Align systems, incentives, budgets and accountability.

Article 02 · Leadership & Legacy

Leadership That Lasts

In brief

The deeper test of leadership is whether people, knowledge and systems continue to grow after a leader leaves.

Leadership is often judged by what we start. Perhaps it should be judged equally by what continues after we leave. Titles are temporary. Institutions are not meant to be.

During our careers, we may lead important initiatives, introduce reforms, build teams, or achieve results that carry our names. But the deeper test of leadership comes later: Did we build something capable of continuing without us?

Sustainable leadership means creating institutions where knowledge is retained, authority is responsibly distributed, people are developed, and good practices become embedded in systems rather than dependent on personalities.

Achievement

What changed while we were there

Legacy

What keeps growing after we leave

Seven Lines · 6 / 22

Leadership & Legacy · Continue the reflection

A different measure of success

This requires a different definition of success. Success is not being indispensable. It is developing people who can surpass us. It is replacing personal intervention with institutional capability. It is turning experience into organizational memory. And it is leaving behind systems strong enough to evolve when circumstances change.

Every leadership position eventually ends. The institution's journey continues. Perhaps, then, one of the most meaningful measures of leadership is not simply what changed while we were there, but what remained capable of growing after we were gone. That is the difference between achievement and legacy.

Every leadership position eventually ends. The institution’s journey continues.

Four foundations for lasting leadership

  • KnowledgeRetain
  • AuthorityDistribute responsibly
  • PeopleDevelop
  • PracticesEmbed in systems

Key takeaways

  • Develop people who can surpass you.
  • Turn experience into institutional memory.
  • Build systems that continue to evolve after you leave.

Article 03 · Strategic Choice

The Discipline to Stop

In brief

Making space for new priorities requires the discipline to remove activities that no longer create value.

One of the most underrated leadership skills is knowing what to stop. Organizations are usually very good at adding. New initiatives. New committees. New reports. New systems. New priorities.

What they are often less disciplined about is removing what no longer creates value. Over time, this creates institutional clutter. People become busy without necessarily becoming more productive. Decision-making slows. Priorities compete with one another. And resources remain tied to activities whose original purpose may have disappeared years ago.

Strategic leadership therefore requires more than deciding what to pursue. It requires the courage to ask: What should we stop doing?

Which process exists only because “we have always done it this way”? Which report is produced but rarely used?

Institutional Clutter

Busy without becoming more productive

Strategic Space

Capacity to pursue new priorities

Seven Lines · 9 / 22

Strategic Choice · Continue the reflection

Create space for priorities

Which approval adds delay without adding meaningful control? Which initiative consumes resources but no longer supports the institution’s priorities?

Stopping something can feel uncomfortable because every activity has an owner, a history, and often a constituency. But strategy is ultimately about choice. An organization that cannot stop old priorities will eventually lose the capacity to pursue new ones. Progress is not always about adding more. Sometimes, the most strategic decision a leader can make is to create space. Before adding the next initiative, perhaps every leadership team should identify one thing it is prepared to stop.

Sometimes, the most strategic decision a leader can make is to create space.

Review Areas

Four places to question old routines

  • ProcessesHabit or purpose?
  • ReportsUsed or filed?
  • ApprovalsControl or delay?
  • InitiativesAligned or outdated?

Key takeaways

  • Review activities that no longer create value.
  • Ask what each process, report and approval contributes.
  • Identify one thing to stop before adding the next initiative.

Article 04 · Performance & Capability

Beyond Today's Results

In brief

A strong institution measures both the results it delivers and the capability it builds for tomorrow.

Most organizations measure performance. Far fewer measure whether they are becoming more capable. Revenue, delivery, compliance, customer satisfaction, project completion and other performance indicators tell us what an institution has achieved. They do not always tell us whether the institution itself is getting stronger.

An organization can deliver excellent results today while quietly becoming more dependent on a few individuals, accumulating outdated processes, losing institutional knowledge, or postponing investments in future capability.

That is why leaders should look beyond performance and ask a second question: Are we becoming a better institution while delivering these results?

Institutional capability can be seen in the quality of decisions, the depth of leadership talent, the reliability of data, the ability to collaborate across boundaries, the speed of learning, and the resilience of systems when circumstances change.

Performance

What did we achieve?

Institutional Capability

What became stronger?

Seven Lines · 12 / 22

Performance & Capability · Continue the reflection

Measure what becomes stronger

These are not secondary considerations. They determine whether today's success can be repeated tomorrow.

The distinction matters particularly in periods of rapid technological, economic and environmental change. An organization optimized only for current performance can become highly efficient at delivering yesterday's priorities. Sustainable excellence requires something more: the continuous renewal of institutional capability. Perhaps every leadership dashboard should therefore contain two dimensions: What did we achieve? and What became stronger because we achieved it? The first measures performance. The second measures whether we are building an institution capable of enduring.

Are we becoming a better institution while delivering these results?

Hidden Risks

Four risks behind strong results

  • DependenceToo few individuals
  • Old processesAccumulation
  • Knowledge lossInstitutional memory
  • Deferred investmentFuture capability

Key takeaways

  • Measure performance and institutional capability together.
  • Look for hidden dependencies, knowledge loss and outdated processes.
  • Ask what became stronger through each achievement.

Article 05 · Resilience & Renewal

Resilience Through Learning

In brief

True resilience turns disruption into intelligence and redesigns the institution before the next shock arrives.

Resilience is not the ability to return to normal. It is the ability to emerge better prepared for what comes next. Too often, resilience is discussed as recovery: restoring operations, rebuilding capacity, and returning to previous levels of performance after disruption.

But returning to the same system that was vulnerable in the first place is not resilience. It is restoration. True resilience requires learning.

After every disruption—economic, environmental, technological, or organizational—leaders should ask: What did this expose about our systems? Which assumptions proved wrong? Where were we too dependent on individuals, suppliers, technologies, or outdated processes? And most importantly: What should we redesign before the next disruption arrives? The strongest institutions do not simply absorb shocks. They convert them into intelligence.

Restoration

Return to previous performance

Resilience

Emerge better prepared

Seven Lines · 15 / 22

Resilience & Renewal · Continue the reflection

Turn shocks into intelligence

They strengthen weak points, diversify critical dependencies, update governance, develop new capabilities, and build greater adaptability into the organization itself.

This distinction matters as the world faces increasingly interconnected challenges—from climate risks and technological disruption to geopolitical and economic uncertainty. The objective should not be to build institutions that never experience disruption. That is unrealistic. The objective is to build institutions that learn faster than the environment changes around them. Resilience is therefore not a defensive capability. It is a strategic one. And sometimes the clearest evidence of resilience is not how quickly an organization returns to where it was—but how intelligently it decides not to.

True resilience requires learning.

Actions

Five actions that build resilience

  • StrengthenWeak points
  • DiversifyDependencies
  • UpdateGovernance
  • DevelopCapabilities
  • AdaptThe institution

Key takeaways

  • Learn from every disruption and question failed assumptions.
  • Redesign weak points before the next shock arrives.
  • Treat resilience as a strategic institutional capability.

Article 06 · Leadership Practice

The Capability Review

In brief

A practical exercise drawn from this month's five articles: examine one decision and choose one change to own.

Choose one recent institutional decision and review it through the five questions below. Invite the people who understand its consequences, including those who will implement it. Use a document, an observed practice or an identified gap to support each answer.

Sustainability: did we consider long-term environmental and social consequences? Continuity: can the work continue if a key person leaves? Focus: what activity could stop so that this priority receives enough attention?

Capability: what became stronger because of the result? Resilience: what should be redesigned in light of the latest lesson or disruption? This is a proposed review exercise, not a performance score or a claim about your institution.

Review Evidence

Document · practice · gap

Make a Commitment

Action · owner · evidence · date

Seven Lines · 18 / 22

Leadership Practice · Continue the reflection

Finish with one owned action

Select one change that addresses the most consequential gap the review revealed. Write the action in a single sentence. Name the person responsible, the evidence that will show whether the change worked, and a date to review that evidence. At the review, record what happened, what you learned and what needs to change next. If the evidence is incomplete, state what is missing. Keep the decision and its lesson together so that the institution retains the knowledge when people or priorities change.

The output is one action, one named owner, clear evidence and a review date. The value lies in the follow-through.

A useful review ends with a change someone owns.

Review Questions

Five lenses. One practical review.

  • SustainabilityConsequences?
  • ContinuityCan work continue?
  • FocusWhat can stop?
  • CapabilityWhat grew stronger?
  • ResilienceWhat needs redesign?

Key takeaways

  • Support each answer with evidence or a clearly identified gap.
  • Choose one action and name its owner.
  • Set a review date and retain the lesson with the decision.

The Leadership Lab

The Decision Challenge

Use only the stated rules. Each challenge has one answer. All planning figures are hypothetical.

  1. Track the process: In each round, close half the open cases, then add 3 new cases. Starting with 54 cases, how many remain after 3 rounds?
  2. Choose within capacity: Each initiative may be chosen once. Choose exactly 2 within 11 team-days. Which pair achieves the highest total priority score? Initiative / Team-days / Priority points: A / 4 / 6; B / 5 / 9; C / 7 / 12.
    InitiativeTeam-daysPriority points
    A46
    B59
    C712
  3. Sequence the reviews: Reviews A, B, C and D run from Monday to Thursday, one per day. A precedes C. B is immediately after D. C is not on Thursday. What is the order?
  4. Separate fact from assumption: Every proposal that passes both the cost and feasibility tests is approved. Proposal X is not approved. Which conclusion must follow? A. It did not pass the cost test. B. It did not pass the feasibility test. C. At least one test was not passed.
Answers and discussion

Answer key: 1 · 12 cases (54 → 30 → 18 → 12). 2 · A + C: 11 team-days, 18 priority points. 3 · Monday A, Tuesday C, Wednesday D, Thursday B. 4 · C: at least one test was not passed.
