Sustainability in Decisions

3 August 2026

Sustainability becomes transformative only when it moves from the margins of strategy to the centre of decision-making.

Too often, sustainability is treated as a separate programme, report, or department.

But the most resilient institutions understand that it must influence how investments are selected, risks are assessed, operations are designed, people are developed, and performance is measured.

A sustainability strategy cannot succeed if the wider organization continues to make decisions using only short-term financial or operational considerations.

Real progress begins when environmental and social impact becomes part of everyday governance.

This means asking better questions:

Will this decision strengthen long-term resilience?

Are we solving a problem—or merely transferring its consequences elsewhere?

Can economic value, environmental responsibility, and social benefit be created together?

The future will not reward organizations that simply communicate ambitious commitments.

It will reward those that embed sustainability into their systems, incentives, budgets, and accountability frameworks.

Sustainability is not an additional responsibility.

It is a better way of making decisions.

Leadership That Lasts

10 August 2026

Leadership is often judged by what we start. Perhaps it should be judged equally by what continues after we leave.

Titles are temporary. Institutions are not meant to be.

During our careers, we may lead important initiatives, introduce reforms, build teams, or achieve results that carry our names. But the deeper test of leadership comes later:

Did we build something capable of continuing without us?

Sustainable leadership means creating institutions where knowledge is retained, authority is responsibly distributed, people are developed, and good practices become embedded in systems rather than dependent on personalities.

This requires a different definition of success.

Success is not being indispensable.

It is developing people who can surpass us.

It is replacing personal intervention with institutional capability.

It is turning experience into organizational memory.

And it is leaving behind systems strong enough to evolve when circumstances change.

Every leadership position eventually ends. The institution's journey continues.

Perhaps, then, one of the most meaningful measures of leadership is not simply what changed while we were there, but what remained capable of growing after we were gone.

That is the difference between achievement and legacy.

The Discipline to Stop

17 August 2026

One of the most underrated leadership skills is knowing what to stop.

Organizations are usually very good at adding.

New initiatives. New committees. New reports. New systems. New priorities.

What they are often less disciplined about is removing what no longer creates value.

Over time, this creates institutional clutter. People become busy without necessarily becoming more productive. Decision-making slows. Priorities compete with one another. And resources remain tied to activities whose original purpose may have disappeared years ago.

Strategic leadership therefore requires more than deciding what to pursue.

It requires the courage to ask:

What should we stop doing?

Which process exists only because “we have always done it this way”?

Which report is produced but rarely used?

August 2026 · 03

Continued

The Discipline to Stop

17 August 2026

Which approval adds delay without adding meaningful control?

Which initiative consumes resources but no longer supports the institution’s priorities?

Stopping something can feel uncomfortable because every activity has an owner, a history, and often a constituency.

But strategy is ultimately about choice.

An organization that cannot stop old priorities will eventually lose the capacity to pursue new ones.

Progress is not always about adding more.

Sometimes, the most strategic decision a leader can make is to create space.

Before adding the next initiative, perhaps every leadership team should identify one thing it is prepared to stop.

August 2026 · 04

Beyond Today's Results

24 August 2026

Most organizations measure performance. Far fewer measure whether they are becoming more capable.

Revenue, delivery, compliance, customer satisfaction, project completion and other performance indicators tell us what an institution has achieved.

They do not always tell us whether the institution itself is getting stronger.

An organization can deliver excellent results today while quietly becoming more dependent on a few individuals, accumulating outdated processes, losing institutional knowledge, or postponing investments in future capability.

That is why leaders should look beyond performance and ask a second question:

Are we becoming a better institution while delivering these results?

Institutional capability can be seen in the quality of decisions, the depth of leadership talent, the reliability of data, the ability to collaborate across boundaries, the speed of learning, and the resilience of systems when circumstances change.

August 2026 · 04

Continued

Beyond Today's Results

24 August 2026

These are not secondary considerations. They determine whether today's success can be repeated tomorrow.

The distinction matters particularly in periods of rapid technological, economic and environmental change. An organization optimized only for current performance can become highly efficient at delivering yesterday's priorities.

Sustainable excellence requires something more: the continuous renewal of institutional capability.

Perhaps every leadership dashboard should therefore contain two dimensions:

What did we achieve?

and

What became stronger because we achieved it?

The first measures performance.

The second measures whether we are building an institution capable of enduring.

August 2026 · 05

Resilience Through Learning

31 August 2026

Resilience is not the ability to return to normal. It is the ability to emerge better prepared for what comes next.

Too often, resilience is discussed as recovery: restoring operations, rebuilding capacity, and returning to previous levels of performance after disruption.

But returning to the same system that was vulnerable in the first place is not resilience.

It is restoration.

True resilience requires learning.

After every disruption—economic, environmental, technological, or organizational—leaders should ask:

What did this expose about our systems?

Which assumptions proved wrong?

Where were we too dependent on individuals, suppliers, technologies, or outdated processes?

And most importantly:

What should we redesign before the next disruption arrives?

The strongest institutions do not simply absorb shocks.

They convert them into intelligence.

August 2026 · 05

Continued

Resilience Through Learning

31 August 2026

They strengthen weak points, diversify critical dependencies, update governance, develop new capabilities, and build greater adaptability into the organization itself.

This distinction matters as the world faces increasingly interconnected challenges—from climate risks and technological disruption to geopolitical and economic uncertainty.

The objective should not be to build institutions that never experience disruption.

That is unrealistic.

The objective is to build institutions that learn faster than the environment changes around them.

Resilience is therefore not a defensive capability.

It is a strategic one.

And sometimes the clearest evidence of resilience is not how quickly an organization returns to where it was—but how intelligently it decides not to.
